01Where this started

A badge that didn't match the chart.

A token on Arc, ticker NOM, showed a "liquidity locked" badge on a major aggregator's token page. Its price chart told a different story: flat, no trades, for hours. Reading the pool contract directly settled it — the position had been removed hours earlier, and nothing was locked. The aggregator's badge was simply stale, or reading the wrong thing.

That discrepancy is the reason this page exists. Not because one badge was wrong once, but because of what it implies: if a major aggregator can misread a Uniswap v4 pool's state, anyone relying on that badge to judge whether a token is safe to buy is being told something the chain does not actually say.

02Why a "locked" badge can be wrong

Uniswap v4 doesn't work like v2, and a lot of tooling still assumes it does.

On Uniswap v2 and most v3 deployments, a liquidity position is an ERC-20 or ERC-721 token you can hold, transfer, or send to a burn address — and "locked" or "burned" is something an indexer can check with a single, simple, well-understood call.

Uniswap v4 does not work that way. There is a single PoolManager contract that holds every pool's tokens for the entire chain — there is no per-pool contract, and no separate LP token. A position is tracked as an entry in the PoolManager's internal accounting, usually wrapped in an NFT held by a position manager contract. Reading "is this locked" now means reading that internal state correctly, for the specific pool, at the current block — not checking a token balance.

The practical effect

A lot of aggregator and scanner tooling was built for v2/v3. Some of it has not caught up to v4's accounting model, or caches a snapshot that stops refreshing once a position changes shape. The result can be a badge that says "locked" against a pool that has already been fully withdrawn. That is a tooling gap, not evidence that any particular badge was placed in bad faith.

The rest of this page is what that gap looks like on-chain, at scale.

03One router, one function, minutes

Every launch we traced used the identical shape.

Following the NOM discrepancy back to its source led to a router contract that a large number of tokens on Arc are launched through: 0x43d894e229a008c72e96872739719b9cfda941d5. Every launch we examined follows the same shape:

  1. Mint. The router mints the full supply (1,000,000,000 tokens in every case we checked) to itself, in the same transaction that creates the token.
  2. Burn and distribute. A portion is sent to the dead address, and small amounts are spread across a long list of wallets — dust-sized, in the cases we checked.
  3. Seed the pool. Most of what's left goes to a wallet that is also, in every case we checked, the address that signed the creation transaction. That wallet forwards its tokens into the Uniswap v4 PoolManager at 0x8366a39cc670b4001a1121b8f6a443a643e40951, opening the pool.
  4. Trade. For a few minutes, real buyers trade against that pool through the router.
  5. Remove. The same wallet that created the token calls the router's removeLiquidity function (selector 0xa0f7978d) on itself, closing the position and paying the underlying tokens back to itself.

Nothing about steps 1–4 is unusual — that is how most direct-pool launches work. Step 5 is the part worth tracing carefully, because it is the same wallet, calling the same function, on the same router, every time.

05The number that repeats: 0.00042

Four different wallets. One identical leftover balance.

After paying out, each of the four wallets above still holds a small amount of Arc's native currency — gas left over from the sequence of transactions. We checked all four current balances. They are not close. They are identical, to the last unit:

Four wallets with no other transaction history in common, launching four different tokens, on two different nights, ending on the exact same fractional balance is not something four independent people do by chance. It is what happens when the same script funds each wallet with a fixed gas budget and executes the identical sequence — create, wait, remove — every time it runs.

The one exception

This time it is not a launch too young to have had time to drain. The single launch that did not clear the threshold (0x9d91e4c89e2e855b733b8e54ab2526331562e777) was 16h 48m old when we took this reading — older than any of the four traced cases in section 04 — and still held 0.0565% of supply in the PoolManager. We are not explaining it away and we are not excluding it from the denominator: it is a genuine outlier, reported as-is rather than dropped.

This is a proxy check, not a transaction-level trace for all 639 — the distinction matters, and section 09 is explicit about it. It is also not the first time we have seen this shape: the four traced cases in section 04 span two separate nights, and the pattern looked the same both times before we ran this wider check.

07A second, different failure mode

Not every version of this problem looks like a removed pool.

We have also looked at launches, on other chains, where the liquidity position itself was never touched — it stayed exactly where it was seeded, held by a burn address, untouchable. On paper, that reads as safe. In practice, a small number of wallets held almost the entire circulating supply outside the pool, and sold into buyers as the price moved. The pool was real and locked; the token was still effectively controlled by a handful of holders who were not the pool.

We are not naming a platform here, deliberately — this is a structural pattern, not an incident report, and we have seen it in more than one place. The point is that "liquidity locked" and "safe to buy" are two different claims. A locked pool says nothing about who holds the rest of the supply.

08What we build differently

Both failure modes, closed at the same point.

Every token launched through ApexPad sends its entire supply — not 79%, not 2.3%, not "most of it" — into the pool at creation. There is no allocation left outside the pool for a small set of wallets to hold and sell into buyers, which closes the failure mode in section 07 by construction: if nobody holds tokens outside the pool, nobody can dump tokens the pool doesn't know about.

For the failure mode in sections 03–06, the position itself is held by a purpose-built contract, LiquidityCustodian.sol, not by the creator's wallet. Its permissions are the whole point:

FunctionWhat it can do

Not present, anywhere in the fileNo decreaseLiquidity(). No burn(). No function that transfers the position NFT out. No selfdestruct. No proxy pattern to route around any of the above.

The function the four wallets in section 04 called — removeLiquidity — has no equivalent path on the custodian. There is no owner key, no admin function, and no upgrade path that adds one back: ownership renounces on-chain, and the missing function stays missing.

ApexPad's own test launch on Arc, FTF, has its position held by custodian 0x119c502a2886d256b84318a8814ff154ab972953. Reading owner() on that contract right now returns 0x0000…0000 — renounced — and the position it holds has not moved since it was seeded. You can read both of those yourself; section 10 shows how.

09What this page does not claim

Said plainly, the way we'd want it said to us.

  • Not an audit. ApexPad's contracts are verified on-chain and have not been reviewed by an independent third party. "Verified" means the published source matches the deployed bytecode — nothing more.
  • Not a full trace of all 639. Sections 04 and 05 are transaction-level, for four cases. Section 06 is a balance check across 639 — a strong proxy, not individual confirmation that the same removeLiquidity call happened for every one of them.
  • Not an accusation against a named company, product or person. We describe on-chain transactions by address and hash, and a general tooling gap in how v4 pool state gets displayed. We do not name the interface used to launch these tokens, and we do not name the platform referenced in section 07.
  • Not investment advice. This page says nothing about whether any token is a good or bad buy, including tokens launched through ApexPad. A locked pool and undiluted supply remove two specific risks; they do not remove the risk that a memecoin goes to zero because nobody wants it.
  • A 6-hour window, not a permanent guarantee. Section 06 describes what we found in one defined window on one chain. We believe it is representative, because the four hand-traced cases span two separate nights and show the same shape — but a new window could look different.

10Check it yourself

Everything above reduces to reads anyone can repeat.

  1. Any of the four tokens — open its contract on Arc's explorer, Transfers tab. You'll see the mint, the burn, the pool seed, and the removal, in that order, all within minutes.
  2. The removal transaction — open it directly. From and the creation transaction's From are the same address. The input data begins 0xa0f7978d.
  3. The residual balance — open any of the four creator wallets in the explorer. The native balance reads 0.00042.
  4. The PoolManager, for any token — call balanceOf(0x8366a39cc670b4001a1121b8f6a443a643e40951) on the token contract's Read Contract tab. A result near zero against a 1,000,000,000-token supply means the pool is effectively empty.
  5. ApexPad's custodian — open 0x119c502a2886d256b84318a8814ff154ab972953 on Arc's explorer, Read Contract, call owner(). Expected answer: 0x0000000000000000000000000000000000000000. Then check Contract for the source: no decreaseLiquidity, no burn, no outbound NFT transfer, anywhere in it.
If any of this doesn't check out

Then this page is wrong, and you'll have found exactly where. Say so — publicly, with the transaction hash. That is the entire reason for writing this as addresses and hashes instead of a claim to take on faith.
